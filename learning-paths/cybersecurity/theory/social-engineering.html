CYBERSECURITY FUNDAMENTALS

Social Engineering

Learn how attackers manipulate people into revealing information, performing actions or bypassing security controls, and understand how users can defend against these techniques.

What is Social Engineering?

Social engineering is the use of psychological manipulation and deception to influence people into revealing information, performing actions or providing access that could compromise security.

Why Social Engineering Matters

Technical security controls cannot completely protect an organization if users are manipulated into making unsafe decisions. Social engineering therefore targets human behavior rather than only technical weaknesses.

  • Helps explain why users can become security targets.
  • Helps organizations improve security awareness.
  • Reduces the risk of unauthorized information disclosure.
  • Supports safer handling of unexpected requests.

Common Social Engineering Techniques

🎣

Phishing

Fraudulent messages attempt to convince users to click links, provide information or perform unsafe actions.

🎭

Pretexting

An attacker creates a false story or identity to make a request appear legitimate.

📞

Vishing

Voice-based social engineering uses phone calls or voice communication to manipulate victims.

💬

Smishing

SMS or messaging platforms are used to deliver deceptive requests or suspicious links.

🚪

Tailgating

An unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted physical area.

🎁

Baiting

An attacker uses an attractive offer, file or physical item to encourage unsafe behavior.

Common Psychological Triggers

Social engineering often relies on normal human reactions and emotions. Attackers may attempt to create pressure so that a person acts before properly verifying a request.

  • Urgency: creating pressure to act immediately.
  • Authority: pretending to be a manager, administrator or official.
  • Fear: suggesting that an account or service will be disabled.
  • Curiosity: encouraging users to open unexpected content.
  • Trust: pretending to be a familiar person or organization.

Simple Example

Example: Fake IT Support Request

An employee receives a phone call from someone claiming to be from the company's IT department. The caller says there is an urgent account problem and asks the employee to provide sensitive account information. Instead of trusting the request immediately, the employee verifies the caller through an official company channel.

How to Defend Against Social Engineering

  • Verify unexpected requests before taking action.
  • Do not share passwords or sensitive information.
  • Be cautious with urgent or unusual requests.
  • Use MFA to add an additional authentication layer.
  • Avoid opening suspicious links or attachments.
  • Contact organizations through trusted channels.
  • Report suspicious activity to the security team.
  • Participate in security-awareness training.

Key Concepts

🧠

Human Factor

Social engineering targets human decisions and behavior rather than relying only on technical vulnerabilities.

🔍

Verification

Confirming unexpected requests through a trusted channel helps prevent manipulation.

Summary

Social engineering uses deception and psychological manipulation to influence people into taking actions that may create security risks. Common techniques include phishing, pretexting, vishing, smishing, baiting and tailgating. Strong awareness, verification, MFA and safe communication practices help reduce social-engineering risks.
